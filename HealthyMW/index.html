VITAL SIGNS MONITORING

Heart rate is the primary indicator of how efficiently the heart pumps oxygenated blood to the brain and tissues, directly reflecting the body's capacity to adapt to physical stress and microgravity. Keeping this parameter within optimal resting values is vital in space because it ensures continuous oxygenation without fatiguing the heart muscle, allows early detection of dehydration or operational stress, and preserves the pumping strength necessary to prevent arrhythmias in orbit or fainting upon returning to Earth's gravity.

Body temperature reflects the balance between heat generated by metabolism and the body's capacity to dissipate it in a gravity-free environment where warm air does not circulate naturally. Keeping this parameter within optimal values is essential in space because it allows all enzymatic reactions and cellular functions to operate with maximum efficiency, timely alerting to infections or thermal failures inside the space suit that could cause severe dehydration, heat stroke, or loss of motor dexterity.

Blood sugar (glycemia) indicates the availability of circulating glucose, which constitutes the primary energy source for the brain, nervous system, and muscle activity. Keeping this parameter within optimal values is vital in space because it guarantees mental clarity, concentration, and the reflexes needed to operate complex systems, preventing episodes of confusion or loss of consciousness due to lack of energy, as well as muscle wear and immune weakening derived from metabolic stress in orbit.

Blood pressure measures the force with which blood circulates through arteries toward vital organs, reflecting the balance between the body's fluid volume and blood vessel tone in microgravity. Keeping this parameter within optimal values is fundamental in space because it ensures constant cerebral perfusion without damaging vascular walls, prevents fainting or extreme fatigue caused by fluid loss in orbit, and avoids excessive intracranial pressure increases that compromise vision during space missions.

Sleep hours measure the physical and neurological recovery time of the organism against the orbital environment, where sunrises every 90 minutes alter natural circadian rhythms. Keeping this parameter within optimal values is vital in space because it consolidates memory, restores attention, and preserves reaction times necessary to avoid human errors in critical operations, in addition to regulating the immune system and preventing mental and hormonal exhaustion during prolonged missions.

Oxygen saturation indicates the percentage of hemoglobin in the blood carrying oxygen to cells, reflecting both pulmonary efficiency and the quality of the artificial atmosphere of the spacecraft or space suit. Keeping this parameter within optimal values is essential in space because it guarantees continuous energy supply to the brain and tissues, allowing immediate detection of life support failures or hypoxia conditions that would compromise the crew's vision, judgment, and survival.

Space radiation is one of the greatest hazards for astronauts traveling far from Earth, such as on missions to Mars. Upon leaving the natural protection of our atmosphere and Earth's magnetic field, crews are exposed to invisible energy originating from solar storms and distant star explosions. This radiation cannot be seen or felt, yet it can damage both spacecraft electronics and the human body, increasing cancer risk and affecting the nervous system, memory, movement, and behavior.

To make long space voyages safe, NASA investigates the effects of this radiation in special Earth-based laboratories simulating space conditions. With this information, scientists develop constant monitoring systems and new protective technologies to shield spacecraft and safeguard astronaut health.

On long voyages like a mission to Mars, astronauts will spend months or years enclosed in very small spaces, far from Earth and without the instant communication we are accustomed to. This extreme confinement, coupled with work overload, lack of sleep, and circadian clock disruption (lacking a normal day/night cycle), can trigger stress, interpersonal friction, health decline, and mission-threatening errors.

To prevent these issues, NASA carefully selects and trains crews, alongside conducting Earth-based simulations (such as the HERA and CHAPEA projects) where volunteers live enclosed for weeks or up to a year mimicking Mars conditions. Through these studies, scientists develop light therapies to regulate sleep, tools for early detection of emotional distress, and activities like keeping personal journals or growing plants to protect astronaut mental health.

Unlike a three-day trip to the Moon, a mission to Mars will take astronauts about 225 million kilometers away from Earth for approximately three years. This vast distance makes immediate help or emergency resupply impossible, causing radio messages to take up to 20 minutes just to arrive. Therefore, if a technical failure or medical emergency occurs, the crew must resolve the problem independently without waiting for real-time instructions from our planet.

To achieve such self-sufficiency, NASA trains astronauts in simulations practicing how to cope with communication delays and strictly ration resources. Furthermore, the agency is developing advanced systems to recycle water and air, as well as nutritious, durable, and varied food items preventing food fatigue, ensuring the crew can survive with onboard provisions.

On a voyage to Mars, astronauts pass through three distinct gravity levels: floating weightless during transit, living with one-third of Earth's gravity on Mars, and re-experiencing normal gravity upon returning to Earth. These abrupt changes disorient the body and impair balance, coordination, and vision, in addition to causing dizziness or fainting upon landing. Without weight-bearing in space, bones lose density each month, muscles weaken, body fluids shift upward toward the head putting pressure on the eyes, and the risk of kidney stones increases.

To prepare the human body and reduce these injuries, NASA studies the effects of weightlessness both on the International Space Station and in specialized Earth facilities. Methods range from head-down tilt bed rest studies to giant pools and cranes simulating partial lunar or Martian gravity, enabling astronaut training and routine design to protect health before and after each landing.

The interior of a spacecraft operates as a closed artificial ecosystem where natural microbes of the human body alter their behavior and transmit much faster among individuals. Compounding this, mission stress weakens astronauts' immune systems, rendering them more prone to allergies or illnesses, while everyday factors like constant noise, poor lighting, cramped space, or uncomfortable temperatures can severely impact daily well-being.

To ensure the crew lives and works safely and comfortably, NASA continuously monitors microorganisms and air quality inside spacecraft and Earth simulators. Moreover, engineers design every centimeter of the habitat—including inflatable modules expanding available space—to guarantee proper pressure, temperature, light, and silence levels, protecting both physical health and astronaut comfort.

Medical Recommendations
Body Temperature
• Level 1: Optimal
Maintain regular ventilation control in habitable modules to ensure proper body heat dissipation.
• Level 2: Caution
Adjust suit or module airflow, cool the torso with a damp compress, and replenish fluids if thermal stress is detected.
• Level 3: Critical
Immediately initiate active cooling measures (in case of hyperthermia/heat stroke) or controlled warming.
Heart Rate
• Level 1: Optimal
Maintain the daily routine of 2.5 hours of aerobic and resistance exercise (ARED and cycle ergometer) to preserve cardiovascular fitness. Maintain scheduled hydration to ensure adequate plasma volume during daily operations on the station.
• Level 2: Caution
Temporarily halt high-concentration tasks, hydrate with electrolytes, and take deep breaths for 15 minutes to stabilize the pulse. Evaluate operational stress levels or current workload, and notify the team if the slight deviation persists after rest.
• Level 3: Critical
Immediately suspend any ongoing physical activity or maneuver, adopt a strict resting position, and avoid sudden movements. Urgently contact the flight surgeon on Earth to activate the hemodynamic stabilization and continuous monitoring protocol.
Oxygen Saturation
• Level 1: Optimal
Routinely verify that life support systems and cabin atmosphere pressure operate within standard parameters. Maintain a slow breathing rhythm and proper posture during daily duties to ensure efficient pulmonary exchange.
• Level 2: Caution
Perform deep breathing cycles, check sensor or oximeter placement, and verify proper ventilation of the workspace. Repeat oximetry measurement after five minutes of relative rest and suspend exertion tasks if the slight drop persists.
• Level 3: Critical
Immediately connect to a supplemental emergency oxygen source and verify the autonomous operation of the suit or module system. Notify the mission control center of the critical hypoxia situation and activate evacuation or leak check protocols for the spacecraft atmosphere.
Blood Pressure
• Level 1: Optimal
Maintain regular fluid intake and the established nutritional plan to balance natural fluid redistribution in microgravity. Continue periodic control logs to ensure vascular tone remains stable against the orbital environment.
• Level 2: Caution
Moderately increase water and salt intake if pressure trends low, or reduce sodium load and evaluate rest if elevated. Take an active break in a well-ventilated space and repeat blood pressure measurement after 30 minutes of relaxation.
• Level 3: Critical
Suspend any critical operations or spacewalks (EVA), remaining in strict rest to prevent syncope or hypertensive crises. Request priority medical tele-assistance with mission control for pharmacological evaluation and clinical management of the emergency.
Blood Sugar (Glycemia)
• Level 1: Optimal
Maintain regular meal schedules and balanced macronutrient intake to sustain stable cognitive performance. Preserve scheduled physical activity to optimize cellular insulin sensitivity against space circadian shifts.
• Level 2: Caution
Consume a fast-absorbing carbohydrate portion if levels drop slightly, or adjust caloric intake and review circadian phase lag if elevated. Perform a new metabolic control measurement fasting or two hours post-meal to confirm glucose stabilization.
• Level 3: Critical
Immediately apply the nutritional rescue protocol with fast-acting glycemic sources in case of severe hypoglycemia, or suspend sugar intake and follow medical guidelines in case of acute hyperglycemia. Report the critical metabolic alteration to mission control to receive instructions on onboard pharmacological or clinical management.
Sleep Duration (Total Sleep Time)
• Level 1: Optimal
Respect the flight plan schedule for continuous rest within the station's sleep compartments. Maintain proper sleep hygiene, correctly utilizing sleeping bags and controlling cabin ambient lighting.
• Level 2: Caution
Adjust LED light exposure (employing red or dimmed tones) to favor natural melatonin production and compensate for circadian disruption. Take a controlled nap or brief relaxation period if nighttime rest was slightly reduced.
• Level 3: Critical
Reschedule operational tasks for the day and immediately suspend any participation in high-risk activities or spacewalks (EVA) due to severe exhaustion. Complete a mandatory rest and sleep period supervised by the medical team to prevent severe operational errors derived from critical fatigue.
